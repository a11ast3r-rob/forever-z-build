# Bottom line

**The overall plan is coherent and worth keeping.**

The major strategy stays:

1. Acquire the right car.
2. Document it exactly as received.
3. Establish engine / clutch / cooling / chassis health.
4. Renew worn components without a parts-cannon rebuild.
5. Measure the actual car.
6. Decide brakes before final wheel clearance is locked.
7. Build air suspension and alignment hardware as one geometry system.
8. Design air-management and audio packaging together.
9. Wrap only after the body is a good substrate.
10. Log everything.

The audit produced a few important refinements, not a redesign.

## Factory 2009 Sport Package
Nissan's 2009 brochure says the Sport Package added:

- larger 4-piston front / 2-piston rear sport brakes
- SynchroRev Match on the 6MT
- VLSD
- front chin spoiler

### Audit conclusion

### Why this matters
If it is non-Sport:
- base brakes are expected,
- open differential is expected,
- SynchroRev Match is not expected,
- Sport hardware is not part of the purchase price.

That does **not** hurt our build concept. We were already planning wheels and suspension. It simply makes a future LSD potentially more valuable and makes the brake decision something to resolve before final wheel purchase.

# 3) Engine health: oil-pressure baseline moves UP the priority list

The factory service information includes a mechanical oil-pressure test procedure: remove the oil-pressure switch, install a gauge, warm the engine, and check pressure under specified conditions.

Enthusiast experience around early VQ37 engines frequently points to internal gallery-gasket problems as one possible cause of low pressure.

### Important distinction
There is no evidence in this audit of a Nissan campaign declaring every early VQ37 gallery gasket defective.

So:
- do **not** open a healthy engine just because it is a 2009,
- but on a 112k-mile 2009 forever car, a properly performed warm mechanical oil-pressure baseline is valuable,
- and abnormal pressure gets diagnosed before modification spending.

### Permanent monitoring
An oil-pressure gauge remains a sensible later monitoring upgrade if we can integrate it cleanly.

# 5) Cooling plan: GREEN, with one new age-related item

## Oil cooler
The early 370Z oil-heat concern is well-supported.

Edmunds' 2009 long-term car received a Nissan-developed auxiliary oil cooler after their Nissan contact advised one for track use.

Later 370Zs received factory oil-cooler hardware.

### Our conclusion
For this forever car:
- monitor factory oil-temperature gauge immediately,
- a **thermostatic external oil cooler** is still a high-value likely upgrade for spirited summer / mountain / track use,
- but it does not excuse skipping engine-health checks.

## Radiator
Do not automatically replace a healthy radiator.

First inspect:
- radiator
- fans
- hoses
- cap
- reservoir
- thermostat behavior
- leaks
- coolant condition
- undertray / airflow

## Heater-hose connector / bleeder
The plastic heater-hose connector near the firewall is widely reported by VQ37 owners and independent platform specialists to become brittle with age. Nissan does not appear to have a recall/TSB for it.

### Our conclusion
At 17 years old:
- inspect it closely,
- if original/brittle or if we are already doing a coolant reset, a quality metal replacement/bleeder is a sensible low-cost longevity item.

This is an enthusiast/age-based prevention item, not a Nissan campaign.

# 6) Steering lock: confirmed official 2009 item

Nissan campaign P3208 / NTB13-014 covers certain 2009–2010 370Zs and addresses a steering-lock assembly condition that can prevent the engine from starting.

### Plan stays
- verify VIN eligibility / campaign completion through Nissan,
- document whether the car has the campaign repair, another repair, or an owner bypass,
- do not simply assume a fuse-pull modification is present or appropriate.

# 7) Manual clutch / CSC: plan is correctly conservative

Owner reports show CSC failures across a wide mileage range. Aftermarket specialists offer external/delete solutions because the factory internal slave is costly to service once the transmission has to come out.

### Audit conclusion
Our current plan is right:
- inspect clutch fluid,
- test pedal return hot and cold,
- watch engagement consistency,
- inspect master-cylinder behavior,
- do not pull a healthy transmission solely out of fear.

If the transmission comes out for clutch/CSC work:
- select the long-term CSC strategy at the same time,
- inspect clutch/flywheel/master-cylinder condition,
- avoid paying the transmission-removal labor twice.

---

# 8) Differential bushing: genuinely high-priority inspection

Z1 documents the large rear differential bushing as fluid-filled and prone to failure, with black streaking on the subframe as a common visual clue. Failure can allow excessive differential movement, thuds/knocks, wheel hop, and increased driveline wear.

### Audit conclusion
Keep it in the Day-1 / first-week chassis inspection.

If failed:
- street urethane is the current default for our car,
- solid aluminum is not the default because this is still a street/highway forever build.

If healthy:
- leave it alone and document it.

---

# 9) Suspension / bushings: no “poly everything”

The plan is internally consistent.

Default:
- OEM-quality rubber where isolation matters,
- serviceable street urethane where geometry/control benefits matter,
- spherical/solid only when a measured performance requirement justifies NVH and upkeep.

Priority inspection:
- front compression/tension-rod bushings
- front upper/lower joints
- inner/outer tie rods
- sway-bar bushings/endlinks
- rear arm/knuckle bushings
- wheel bearings
- engine/trans mounts
- rear subframe bushings

### Rear subframe
Do not rebuild it simply because the car is old. Replace only if condition/play/noise/alignment stability warrants it.

The hardware direction is valid, but fabrication/body modification must be treated as part of the job plan.

Our management architecture remains good:

- height + pressure management
- dual electric compressors
- two smaller hidden tanks as **one shared reserve**
- check valves
- accessible drains
- compressor ventilation
- protected wiring/airline
- DRIVE / LIFT / LOW presets
- alignment at DRIVE height

# 11) Rear toe correction: our compatibility rule is CONFIRMED

Z1 explicitly says its adjustable rear toe arm replaces the factory spring arm and is for **true-style rear coilovers only**; it does not apply when the original spring bucket is retained.

Air Lift's rear kit uses the rear spring location.

SPC 72265 officially fits 2009–2020 370Z and provides extra rear camber/toe adjustment by replacing the OE cams; the factory slots need to be elongated for the added range.

### Audit conclusion
For our Air Lift layout:
- do not buy a spring-bucket-delete true-style toe arm,
- use spring-bucket-compatible toe correction,
- SPC 72265 remains a valid candidate,
- final camber/toe hardware gets selected from the actual drive-height alignment results.

Z1's street arms also match our NVH goal: their street variants use urethane bushings, while race variants use solid/heim joints with more noise/vibration.

# 12) Wheels: wheel specs are strong; tire size stays YELLOW until final measurement

## Wheels
The intended 57DR dimensions:
- 18×9.5 +22 front
- 18×10.5 +12 rear

are well represented on real 370Z builds.

A real 2016 370Z example runs exactly those 57DR wheel specs with:
- 255/40R18 front
- 285/35R18 rear
- BC Racing coilovers
- no trimming
- no rubbing
- no front spacer
- 5 mm rear spacer

Another 370Z Base gallery also documents the exact +22/+12 57DR wheel pair without rubbing on coilovers.

### But
Neither proves Air Lift clearance on **our** car.

## Tire-diameter gut check
Compared with factory Base:
- stock front 225/50R18 ≈ 26.86 in
- stock rear 245/45R18 ≈ 26.68 in
- target front 255/40R18 ≈ 26.03 in
- target rear 285/35R18 ≈ 25.85 in

So the target pair is about **3.1% shorter overall** than the Base factory tires.

Good news:
- the target front/rear diameter relationship is almost identical to the stock relationship, which is desirable for the car's stagger-sensitive systems.

Tradeoff:
- slightly shorter effective gearing
- speedometer will read somewhat high
- more wheel-well gap at a given suspension height

### Audit conclusion
Keep 255/40 + 285/35 as a **real, proven target**, not a purchase lock.

At final fitment we compare:
- 255/40 + 285/35
- a slightly taller/meatier alternative
- tire availability
- Air Lift clearance
- brake clearance
- desired DRIVE-height look

# 13) Brakes: make the decision before final wheel order

If this car is the non-Sport Base configuration indicated by the listing, it likely starts with the smaller standard brakes.

For street use, healthy base brakes with:
- good pads
- good rotors
- fresh fluid

are not an automatic problem.

A Sport/Akebono-style upgrade remains a good OEM+ option for:
- thermal capacity
- appearance
- future hard use

### Audit conclusion
Before buying the 57DRs:
1. decide whether Akebonos are a likely near-term goal,
2. verify the exact wheel-face/caliper clearance for the chosen 57DR spec,
3. then lock wheels.

Do not buy brakes simply for the checklist.

# 14) Differential / LSD: potentially one of our best future “fun” upgrades

The 2009 factory brochure makes VLSD part of the Sport Package.

If the candidate proves to be a plain non-Sport Base:
- verify it still has the original/open differential,
- do not assume no previous owner changed it.

If it is open and the car is healthy, a quality street-oriented LSD may add more usable fun than chasing small naturally aspirated horsepower gains.

For a quiet highway car, a helical/gear-type unit is worth considering later versus a more aggressive clutch-type setup.

This stays a future decision, not a Day-1 purchase.

# 17) One additional “listen for it” item

Nissan has a later bulletin for axle clicking during takeoff/acceleration on 2010+ 370Zs. The bulletin's formal 370Z coverage starts at 2010, not our 2009 candidate.

So we do **not** label it an official 2009 issue.

Still, during our driveline baseline, listen for:
- click/pop at takeoff
- clunk on throttle reversal

and diagnose the actual source rather than automatically ordering axles.

If the car passes purchase inspection, money goes in this order:

# Audit verdict

## GREEN — keep as planned
- forever-car philosophy
- inspection-first workflow
- documentation
- hidden two-tank shared air reserve
- dual compressors
- ALP4 + optional height sensing
- street-bushed alignment hardware
- spring-bucket-compatible rear toe strategy
- align at DRIVE height
- oil cooler concept
- audio + air co-design
- body prep before wrap
- no poly/solid-everything approach

## YELLOW — correct idea, final decision waits for the car
- 255/40 + 285/35 tire sizes
- Akebono conversion
- LSD
- exact camber/toe/traction arms
- oil-cooler size
- tank sizes/locations
- subwoofer size/count
- wrap color
- exhaust / tune path

## RED — do not do
- skip the PPI because the price looks fair
- assume the car is Sport-equipped
- assume the gallery gasket is bad without pressure evidence
- delete the rear spring bucket with an incompatible toe arm
- buy every bushing in polyurethane
- solid-mount the chassis for a highway build
- buy wheels before deciding brake clearance
- wrap over unresolved body/paint failure
- bury unknown audio wiring under a new false floor
